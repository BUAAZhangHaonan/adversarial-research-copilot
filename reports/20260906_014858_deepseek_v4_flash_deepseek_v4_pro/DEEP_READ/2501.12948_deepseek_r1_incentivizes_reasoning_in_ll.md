# DeepSeek-R1 incentivizes reasoning in LLMs through reinforcement learning

- arxiv: 2501.12948
- venue: Nature

## Extraction

This paper is highly relevant to the question. Below, I extract the requested content, explicitly labeling author-stated content vs. my own inference.

# (1) VERIFIED CLAIMS

## What was demonstrated, and under which conditions

**Author-stated claim: Pure RL (no SFT) improves reasoning on a base model (§2.2, §2.2.4, Figures 2–3, Tables 2–3)**

- DeepSeek-R1-Zero applies GRPO RL directly to DeepSeek-V3-Base with **no supervised fine-tuning** (§2.2.1, §1).
- **Rewards used**: rule-based accuracy rewards (deterministic math answers verified by format rules; LeetCode problems verified by a compiler) + a format reward enforcing `<think>…</think>` tags. **No outcome/process neural reward model was used**, explicitly because of reward-hacking and pipeline-complexity concerns (§2.2.2).
- **Template**: only structural constraints (“first produce a reasoning process, then the final answer”); the authors state they deliberately avoided content-specific biases such as mandating reflection (§2.2.3, Table 1).
- **Data distribution**: reasoning questions with rule-verifiable answers (math with deterministic answers, LeetCode/code with predefined test cases) (§2.2.2).
- **Scale reported**: ~8,500 RL steps (Figure 2/3 x-axes); authors do not report FLOPs, GPU-hours, batch sizes, or learning rates.
- **Result**: AIME 2024 pass@1 rises from 15.6% → 71.0% during training; with majority voting (cons@64) 86.7%, exceeding OpenAI-o1-0912's 83.3% (§2.2.4; Table 2). MATH-500 95.9%, GPQA Diamond 73.3% (Table 2).
- Emergent behaviors (self-verification, reflection, longer CoT, the “aha moment”) are documented qualitatively and anecdotally (Table 3; Figure 3 shows average response length growing to ~9,500 tokens by step 8,500).
- Evaluation protocol (§3): non-greedy sampling (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, k responses typically 4–64), pass@1 = (1/k)Σpᵢ; AIME additionally cons@64 from 64 samples. Greedy decoding was explicitly avoided because it produces “higher repetition rates and significant variability across different checkpoints” (§3).

**Author-stated claim: Adding cold-start SFT + multi-stage training further improves performance and readability (§2.3, Tables 2 vs 4)**

- DeepSeek-R1 pipeline: (a) cold-start SFT on “thousands” of long-CoT samples; (b) reasoning-oriented RL (same GRPO + rule-based rewards + a **language-consistency reward**, which the authors note causes a “slight degradation” in performance in ablations); (c) rejection sampling from the RL checkpoint to build ~600k reasoning SFT samples + ~200k non-reasoning samples from the DeepSeek-V3 pipeline, then fine-tune two epochs on ~800k samples; (d) a second RL stage for helpfulness/harmlessness reasoning (rule-based rewards for reasoning data; reward models for general data) (§2.3.1–2.3.4).
- Results: AIME 2024 79.8%, MATH-500 97.3%, Codeforces percentile 96.3, SWE-bench Verified 49.2%, vs reported OpenAI-o1-1217: 79.2 / 96.4 / 96.6 / 48.9 (Table 4, Figure 1). GPQA Diamond is lower than o1-1217 (71.5 vs 75.7). Knowledge benchmarks (MMLU 90.8, MMLU-Pro 84.0) beat DeepSeek-V3 but are below reported o1-1217 (91.8) (§1.2, Table 4).
- Condition to note: **OpenAI-o1-1217 numbers are not measured by the authors** — “Since accessing the OpenAI-o1-1217 API is challenging in mainland China, we report its performance based on official reports” (§3, Baselines paragraph).

**Author-stated claim: Distillation of R1 into smaller dense models via SFT-only works well (§2.4, §3.2, Table 5)**

- Base models: Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B/7B, Qwen2.5-14B/32B, Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct; SFT only on the ~800k curated samples — **no RL was applied to distilled models** (§2.4).
- Results: Distill-Qwen-32B: AIME 72.6%, MATH-500 94.3%, LiveCodeBench 57.2%, GPQA 62.1% (Table 5); Distill-Llama-70B: AIME 70.0% pass@1 / 86.7% cons@64 (Table 5).

## What the evaluation does NOT rule out (inference)

- **o1 comparisons are not controlled**: The headline parity claims with o1-1217/o1-0912 rely on OpenAI's official reported scores obtained under an unknown, non-identical harness/prompt protocol (§3). Differences of 1–3 points (e.g., AIME 79.8 vs 79.2; GPQA 71.5 vs 75.7) are within the margin of uncertainty this introduces.
- **Benchmark protocol is not matched across baseline models**: The authors changed MMLU-Pro, C-Eval, and CLUE-WSC prompts to zero-shot because “the CoT in few-shot may hurt the performance of DeepSeek-R1” (§3, Evaluation Prompts). Baseline scores from other models may use different (few-shot) settings; this makes even in-paper comparisons (e.g., vs DeepSeek-V3) not strictly apples-to-apples.
- **Contamination is not analyzed**: No contamination check or discussion is presented for AIME/GPQA/Codeforces datasets, except citing LiveCodeBench as “contamination free” (§3). The possibility that RL training data overlaps benchmark distributions is not ruled out, especially since AIME problems have public years of released exams.
- **R1-Zero's “pure RL” is not supervision-free in a strong sense**: The base model DeepSeek-V3-Base is already a highly capable model (initial AIME 15.6% without any RL, §2.2.4). The template (§2.2.3), the choice of rule-verifiable problems, and the accuracy-reward design encode human task knowledge. Thus “reasoning emerges from RL alone” is demonstrated only conditional on a large, capable pretrained base and heavy human reward/task design.
- **The cause of the R1 vs R1-Zero gap is confounded**: R1 differs from R1-Zero by (i) cold-start SFT data, (ii) language-consistency reward, (iii) 800k additional SFT data, and (iv) a second RL stage, and the two models are not compared under a matched compute/step budget (§2.3 vs §2.2; Table 2 vs Table 4). No ablation isolates cold-start SFT as the driver.
- **R1-Zero's general reasoning is narrow**: It trails o1-mini on LiveCodeBench (50.0 vs 53.8) and lags both o1 models badly on Codeforces rating (1444 vs 1820/1843) (Table 2). So “pure RL incentivizes reasoning” is verified mainly for math-competition-style tasks.
- **“Aha moment”/reflection claims are anecdotal**: They rest on a single illustrative generation (Table 3) and aggregate length curves (Figure 3), not on causal analysis of which behaviors drive accuracy gains.
- **Distillation vs. RL comparison is not fully controlled**: Table 6 compares Distill-Qwen-32B (SFT on teacher-generated 800k samples) vs “DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B” (pure RL >10k steps on math/code/STEM data). The two runs differ in data source, data curation, and reward design, not only in method (distillation vs RL); compute is not reported for either. Thus “distillation beats RL for small models” is demonstrated only for this specific setup.
- **Factual/refusal costs**: R1 underperforms DeepSeek-V3 on C-SimpleQA (63.7 vs 68.0), attributed by the authors to safety-RL refusals (§3.1); R1's SimpleQA (30.1) is far below reported o1-1217 (47.0) (Table 4).

# (2) AUTHOR-STATED LIMITATIONS

**Section 5 (“Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work”) states four limitations directly:**

1. **General Capability**: “Currently, the capabilities of DeepSeek-R1 fall short of DeepSeek-V3 in tasks such as function calling, multi-turn, complex role-playing, and JSON output.” The authors say they plan to explore using long CoT for these fields.
2. **Language Mixing**: “DeepSeek-R1 is currently optimized for Chinese and English, which may result in language mixing issues when handling queries in other languages. For instance, DeepSeek-R1 might use English for reasoning and responses, even if the query is in a language other than English or Chinese.”
3. **Prompting Engineering**: “When evaluating DeepSeek-R1, we observe that it is sensitive to prompts. Few-shot prompting consistently degrades its performance. Therefore, we recommend users directly describe the problem and specify the output format using a zero-shot setting for optimal results.”
4. **Software Engineering Tasks**: “Due to the long evaluation times, which impact the efficiency of the RL process, large-scale RL has not been applied extensively in software engineering tasks. As a result, DeepSeek-R1 has not demonstrated a huge improvement over DeepSeek-V3 on software engineering benchmarks.”

**Additional limitations stated elsewhere:**

- **DeepSeek-R1-Zero readability/language mixing** (stated in §2.2.4 and §2.3.1): R1-Zero “struggles with challenges like poor readability, and language mixing”; its responses “may mix multiple languages or lack markdown formatting.”
- **o1 baselines not independently measured** (implied limitation of the comparison, §3): “we report its performance based on official reports.”
- **Reasoning-oriented RL is limited to rule-verifiable domains**: “This phase focuses on enhancing the model’s reasoning capabilities, particularly in reasoning-intensive tasks… which involve well-defined problems with clear solutions” (§2.3.2). For the second RL stage, general-domain data requires reward models because rule-based rewards do not apply (§2.3.4).
- **Neural PRMs and MCTS were tried and abandoned** (§4.2): PRM limitations: hard to define fine-grained steps in general reasoning, hard to label intermediate-step correctness, automated annotation unsatisfactory/manual annotation not scalable, and model-based PRMs “inevitably lead to reward hacking.” MCTS limitations: token generation has an exponentially large search space; node-expansion limits cause local optima; fine-grained value models are too hard to train for iterative self-improvement.
- **Chinese SimpleQA regression** (§3.1): “DeepSeek-R1 performs worse than DeepSeek-V3 on the Chinese SimpleQA benchmark, primarily due to its tendency to refuse answering certain queries after safety RL. Without safety RL, DeepSeek-R1 could achieve an accuracy of over 70%.” (This is stated as a diagnostic rather than framed as a limitation, but it is a functional limitation of the released model.)

# (3) OPENED QUESTIONS

## A. Future work the authors explicitly call for

From §5:
- Using long CoT to enhance **function calling, multi-turn interaction, complex role-playing, and JSON output**.
- Addressing **language mixing** for languages beyond Chinese and English.
- **Prompting robustness** (they note the sensitivity and recommend zero-shot, implying prompt-engineering research).
- Scaling RL to **software engineering tasks**, via rejection sampling on SWE data or asynchronous evaluations in RL.

From §3.2 and §2.4 (distillation discussion):
- The authors observed that “applying RL to these distilled models yields significant further gains… warrants further exploration” (§3.2), and they deliberately “leave the exploration of the RL stage to the broader research community” (§2.4).

From §1.1 / §4.1 (broader agenda):
- “Advancing beyond the boundaries of intelligence may still require more powerful base models and larger-scale reinforcement learning” (§4.1).
- The paper frames test-time scaling via longer CoT as an open problem for the field (§1).

## B. Questions their verified claims leave unanswered but the authors do not mention (inference)

- **Generalization mechanism**: Why does RL on math/code improve non-reasoning domains (writing, AlpacaEval 87.6% LC win-rate, ArenaHard 92.3%)? The paper reports this as a benefit of “large-scale RL” (§3.1) but never tests whether it is the CoT capability, the SFT data mixture, or something else driving it.
- **Reward-hacking ceiling for rule-based rewards**: The authors reject neural PRMs because of reward hacking (§2.2.2, §4.2), but do not discuss whether their own accuracy/format rule-based rewards were monitored for gaming over long training horizons (e.g., producing plausible but unverifiable reasoning that happens to yield the right boxed answer).
- **Compute-matched comparison of pure RL vs cold-start RL**: Given their claim that cold-start data improves convergence/performance (§2.3.1), no experiment isolates the cold-start effect with matched compute, data, and rewards. The unanswered question is whether pure RL simply needs more steps or genuinely saturates lower.
- **Length trade-offs**: Average responses grow to ~9,500 tokens by step 8,500 (Figure 3); R1 cap is 32,768 tokens (§3). There is no analysis of how much of the gain comes from longer outputs vs. better per-token reasoning, and no cost/latency analysis — important for deployment but absent.
- **Under what conditions distillation beats RL**: Is the Table 6 result driven by model scale, RL instability on small models, data quality/curation differences, or the teacher's capability? Only one small-model RL run (Qwen-32B) is shown, so the scope of the conclusion is unknown.
- **Contamination and memorization**: No contamination analysis is provided (see §1 above), so the extent to which benchmark gains reflect genuine out-of-distribution reasoning is unknown.
- **Safety of hidden CoT**: The harmlessness reward evaluates the entire response including reasoning (§2.3.4), but the paper does not explore whether long hidden reasoning traces enable or obscure jailbreaks, nor whether users can/should inspect CoT.
- **Behavioral persistence**: The “aha moment” and reflection behaviors are observed in training; whether they persist under distribution shift, shorter output budgets, or fine-tuning for downstream tasks is untested.

# (4) LOAD-BEARING ASSUMPTIONS

These are assumptions that the method or conclusions require; newer evidence could invalidate them. (Most are inferences; where the authors state or presuppose them, I note that.)

1. **Rule-based accuracy/format rewards are a sufficient training signal for “reasoning”** (§2.2.2). The core claim that RL incentivizes reasoning presupposes that deterministic-answer verification (math, LeetCode) induces general reasoning rather than task-specific pattern-matching or reward hacking. If rule-based rewards are gameable at scale (the very concern the authors raise for PRMs), the R1-Zero result would be reinterpreted.
2. **The base model contributes no relevant supervision (“pure RL” claim, §1, §2.2).** The headline contribution—“reasoning capabilities… incentivized purely through RL without SFT”—assumes that a strong pretrained base (DeepSeek-V3-Base) does not already contain most of the reasoning skill, with RL merely selecting it. A weaker or differently pretrained base could fail to show the same emergence.
3. **Emergent behaviors (reflection, self-verification, “aha moment”) are genuine cognitive strategies, not artifacts of the reward/format shaping** (§2.2.4, Table 3). The authors interpret these as natural self-evolution; the alternative — that format rewards and benchmark distributions select for verbose self-correction patterns — is not ruled out by the presented aggregate/anecdotal evidence.
4. **Benchmark performance is a valid proxy for reasoning capability, and benchmark/test-set contamination is absent or negligible** (§3). The authors use standard benchmarks (AIME, MATH-500, GPQA Diamond, Codeforces, LiveCodeBench) with no contamination analysis. If training data overlaps these datasets (especially public competition problems), scores overstate generalization.
5. **The reported evaluation protocol (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, pass@1 over k samples, §3) is unbiased and comparable across models.** This assumption is load-bearing for all cross-model claims. It is especially fragile for o1 comparisons, since the authors did not run o1 themselves but cite official reports (§3).
6. **Zero-shot prompting is the fair common evaluation mode** (§3). The authors modify few-shot benchmarks to zero-shot because few-shot CoT “hurts” R1; this presumes the modified setting is not differentially favorable to R1 relative to baselines.
7. **The 800k curated SFT dataset (600k reasoning + 200k non-reasoning) is a faithful transfer vehicle for reasoning** (§2.3.3, §2.4). Distillation conclusions assume teacher-generated trajectories filtered by rule-based rewards and DeepSeek-V3 generative judgments are correct, representative, and sufficient to teach smaller models reasoning patterns. If the generative reward model is biased or the filtered set has hidden distributional skew, the distillation results could largely reflect data curation rather than the teacher's reasoning patterns.
8. **Language-consistency reward measures readability without materially hurting reasoning** (§2.3.2). The authors note it causes a “slight degradation” in ablations; the assumption is that the degradation is acceptable and that target-language-word proportion is a valid proxy for language mixing/readability.
9. **Distillation-vs-RL conclusion generalizes** (§4.1, Table 6). The conclusion that smaller models get more from distillation than from large-scale RL assumes Qwen-32B is representative of small models, that the two training runs are otherwise comparable, and that this ordering holds across scales, data mixes, and compute budgets. Only one RL run on one small model is reported.
10. **RL for helpfulness/harmlessness does not undermine reasoning** (§2.3.4). The final RL stage mixes rule-based and preference-reward signals; the paper assumes the two objectives do not interfere destructively. The C-SimpleQA regression (safety RL causing refusals, §3.1) shows such interference does occur in at least one dimension, undermining the generality of this assumption.
11. **OpenAI-o1-1217 official numbers are trustworthy and comparable** (§3). The headline “comparable to o1-1217” claim depends entirely on external reported values under unspecified evaluation conditions — an assumption that could be invalidated by independent re-evaluation under identical protocols.
