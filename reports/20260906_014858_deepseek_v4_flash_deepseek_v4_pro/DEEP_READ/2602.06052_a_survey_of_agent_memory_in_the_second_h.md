# A Survey of Agent Memory in the Second Half: Towards Self-Evolving and Long-Horizon Agents

- arxiv: 2602.06052
- venue: arXiv.org

## Extraction

**Important epistemic caveat before the four sections.** This paper is a *survey / position paper*, not an experimental study. It contains no new experiments, datasets, or benchmarks of its own. Consequently, "verified claims" below are of two kinds: (a) claims the survey itself makes about the literature (bibliometric counts, taxonomy coverage, benchmark deficits) and (b) claims the survey *reports* about individual systems (e.g., "MemGPT formalizes memory hierarchies"), which the survey does not independently re-verify. I mark each item **[A] author-stated**, **[I] inference**, or **[A→I]** where I extend an author claim into an implication. Section/figure citations refer to the paper's own numbering.

One concrete anomaly worth noting in advance: the text says the corpus covers "2023 Q1 and 2025 Q4" (§3), but Figure 3's data bins extend to "2026-Q1," and some references are dated 2026 (e.g., Bei et al., 2026, arXiv 2601.03515). This may be a markup artifact, but it means the stated time-window is not strictly reproducible from the paper's own exhibits.

### (1) VERIFIED CLAIMS

**What the survey demonstrates about the literature itself**

- **[A] Corpus construction.** The survey's empirical base is "218 key articles published between 2023 Q1 and 2025 Q4," curated from "an initial pool of several hundred papers" by Google Scholar keyword search (agent memory, long-term memory, context management, personalization memory, etc.) plus manual screening of "top-tier NLP, ML, IR, and AI venues" (§3, "Taxonomy of Memory in Foundation Agents"). *Controlled variables:* time window, venue filter, keyword set. *Not controlled:* no PRISMA-style exclusion log, no inter-annotator reliability check, no replication of the search by independent coders; "most relevant" is an author judgment (§3).
- **[A] Growth trend.** "The publication volume witnesses an exponential surge throughout 2025, culminating in a significant peak in Q4" (Figure 3). The figure's final counts are 185 external vs. 33 internal substrates; 72 user-centric vs. 146 agent-centric subjects. **[I]** These exact totals are artifacts of the authors' coding decision that every paper receives exactly one substrate label and one subject label (185+33 = 218 and 72+146 = 218), whereas cognitive-mechanism labels sum to >218 (25+89+73+35+86 = 308), implying multi-labeling. This labeling scheme is not justified or tested in the paper.
- **[A] Agent-centric work dominates.** "Working memory, procedural memory, and sensory memory are predominantly agent-centric… Semantic and episodic memory appear in both user- and agent-centric settings" (Figure 5, §3.3). **Conditions:** This is a claim about *the authors' own classification*, not an independently auditable fact.

**Claims about the design space (taxonomy)**

- **[A] Five atomic cognitive mechanisms.** "These five systems constitute a minimal and architecturally complete decomposition of cognitive memory, whereas other memory constructs such as autobiographical or prospective memory can be understood as compositions or functional abstractions built upon them" (§3.2). This is an architectural assertion, not a demonstrated result; no proof of completeness or atomicity is given.
- **[A] Operation mechanics.** Single-agent memory is decomposed into five operations — storage & index, loading & retrieval, update & refresh, compression & summarization, forgetting & retention — and multi-agent memory into architecture, routing, and isolation/conflict mechanisms (Figure 6, §4.1–4.2, with examples: Memory-R1's ADD/UPDATE/DELETE/NOOP actions, EvoMem's verifier scoring, hybrid private/shared memory in Collaborative Memory and MirrorMind, etc.).
- **[A] Three learning-policy paradigms.** Prompt-based, fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning over memory operations (§5), with RL claims framed as "progressively extending the temporal scope over which reinforcement signals shape memory behavior" (§5.3).

**Claims about benchmark coverage and evaluation (the survey's most concrete "empirical" content)**

- **[A] Benchmark inadequacy.** "The majority of LLM and agent benchmarks today are still configured in context-limited simple environments… instance-isolated… reset-centric… short evaluation horizons" (§6.1; Table 4). This is supported by the authors' own tabulation (e.g., WebArena 812 tasks, OSWorld 369 tasks) but the table does not itself prove horizon length — the categorization is the authors' judgment. **[I]** The claim "strong benchmark performance often reflects proficiency in in-context pattern matching or short-term reasoning, rather than the ability to accumulate, retain, and reuse knowledge" (§6.1) is *not* demonstrated; the paper does not present controlled evidence that memory, rather than planning, grounding, or perception, is the missing capability in these settings. It is a diagnosis, not a result.
- **[A] User-centric benchmark weaknesses.** Early benchmarks (MSC, DuLeMon) "rely heavily on similarity-based or generated-based metrics (e.g., BLEU, Distinct-n, Perplexity, ROUGE), which weakly isolate memory… making FE, UR, and FR difficult to attribute" (§7.2.1, Table 3); newer benchmarks (MemoryBank, HaluMem, LongMemEval) "increasingly move toward mechanism-attributable evaluation" (§7.2.1).
- **[A] Coverage gaps.** "CS [Compression/Summarization] and FR [Forgetting/Retention] remain comparatively under systematically evaluated in the current benchmark designs… AB [Abstain & Boundary Handling] is also inconsistently required" (§7.2.1). This is directly checkable against their own Table 3 marks (e.g., CS is ✗ in HaluMem, PrefEval, ConvoMem; AB is ✗ in MSC, DuLeMon, MemoryBank, PersonaMem, MemBench).
- **[A] Procedural memory evaluation lag.** "Procedural memory evaluation remains less standardized than fact-centric settings" (§3.2.5).
- **[A] Judge-dependent evaluation risk.** LLM-as-a-judge "introduces judge dependence and sensitivity to prompting" (§7.1).

**Claims reported about specific systems (not re-verified)**

- **[A] Representative system claims as the survey reports them:** MemGPT "explicitly formalize[s] memory hierarchies to manage long-term conversational state" (§3.3.1); Memory-R1 learns ADD/UPDATE/DELETE/NOOP memory edits via RL (§4.2.3, §5.3.1); Mem-α "frame[s] memory construction itself as a sequential decision-making problem" learned via RL (§5.3.1); MEM1 maintains "near-constant memory usage across arbitrarily long, multi-turn interactions" and MemAgent scales long-text processing "with linear complexity and minimal performance degradation" (§3.3.2). **Conditions:** the survey does not report the base models, compute budgets, training data distributions, or full evaluation protocols of these works; it selectively summarizes them. Their efficacy is attested only by the underlying papers. **[I]** I did not (and the survey did not) audit those primary results; confounds reported in the original papers (e.g., base-LLM choice, prompt template, benchmark overlap with pretraining data) are not controlled for in the survey's synthesis.

**Alternative explanations the survey's evaluation does NOT rule out**

- **[I] Selection bias.** The exponential trend and category distributions could be driven largely by keyword/citation inflation (papers branding themselves as "memory" for visibility) and by the authors' subjective "iterative screening," not by a real shift in the field's functional contributions. No independent audit exists.
- **[I] Confounded efficacy attribution.** When the survey claims memory mechanisms improve task completion (e.g., "WebArena benchmarking demonstrates that episodic memory… substantially outperforms baselines," §8), it does not control for total token budget, inference cost, or base-model capability differences. The improvement could come from extra compute/context rather than from memory *qua* memory.
- **[I] The "utility gap" argument is not closed.** The paper asserts agents fail in real-world long-horizon settings because current benchmarks don't test memory (§6.1), but it never tests whether memory-equipped agents *actually outperform* strong no-memory baselines (e.g., very long-context models, state-space models, or better planners) in those settings. The central motivating claim is therefore plausible but unverified.
- **[A→I] Even the newer "long-context" benchmarks are acknowledged exceptions** (e.g., LongMemEval up to 1.5M tokens; ConvoMem 3M; PerLTQA 1M in Table 3), so the "majority context-limited" claim is never quantified as a fraction of benchmarks surveyed.

### (2) AUTHOR-STATED LIMITATIONS

**The paper has no dedicated "Limitations" section.** The limitations the authors state are overwhelmingly limitations *of the field, of the surveyed methods, or of benchmarks* — not self-critique of the survey. The closest author-stated statements:

- **[A] Sensory memory is largely unmodeled.** "Sensory memory is typically not explicitly modeled" (§3.2.1) because textual inputs are already abstracted; only "a limited number of LLM agent works explicitly instantiate sensory memory as a distinct stage" (HMT, LightMem, M2PA), with implicit versions in streaming/embodied systems (§3.2.1).
- **[A] External-memory drawbacks.** Inference latency ("Normally, inference takes a longer time since the agent has to retrieve information from external storage"); retrieval unreliability ("If the similarity or ranking algorithms are not well-developed, they may provide results that are irrelevant or unhelpful for the current task… [introducing] noise that may reduce the agent's utility"); escalating storage/indexing cost ("As the system operates for longer periods… the escalating costs of storage and indexing may further diminish efficiency and performance") (§3.1.1).
- **[A] Parametric-memory drawbacks.** "Modifying weights is computationally costly and may introduce interference, overwriting, or distortion of previously learned knowledge" (§3.1.2); latent-state memory is "ephemeral… typically discarded or reset at the end of a request" and scales with "model depth, batch size, and precision"; the KV cache comes "at the cost of higher memory usage and implementation complexity" (§3.1.2).
- **[A] Text-record and hierarchical-store costs.** Text records "require meticulous summarization and pruning to maintain a succinct core summary and manageable lists"; hierarchical stores add "architectural complexity… latency and additional resource cost… and requires well-designed mechanisms to maintain data consistency and synchronize updates" (§3.1.1).
- **[A] No dominating substrate.** "No single substrate dominates across settings and environments" (§3.1.3).
- **[A] Prompt-based memory policies are weak at credit assignment.** Dynamic prompt-based control methods "remain fundamentally language-mediated and lack explicit credit assignment, limiting their capacity for long-term policy optimization compared to fine-tuning and reinforcement learning–based approaches" (§5.1.2).
- **[A] SFT-based retrieval policies are frozen.** "The resulting retrieval policies remain fixed after training and do not incorporate explicit credit assignment over extended decision horizons" (§5.2.3).
- **[A] Long-horizon dialogue context failure.** Mitigation strategies "improve scalability but often degrade recall reliability… exposing a fundamental trade-off between context budget control and long-term information accessibility" (§6.2.1). Tool-use contexts "grow linearly or even exponentially with interaction length," and "naively removing or compressing these traces risks breaking causal dependencies between reasoning and action steps" (§6.2.1).
- **[A] Flattening structured environment state is lossy.** "Naively flattening these artifacts into token sequences is both inefficient and structurally lossy, undermining interpretability, precise retrieval, and targeted updates" (§6.2.2).
- **[A] Metric limitations.** Similarity metrics "can underestimate valid paraphrases and overestimate fluent yet ungrounded responses"; perplexity "does not verify whether the model's content is grounded in the correct historical evidence" (§7.1). Memory failures "may not change end-task accuracy until they accumulate" (§7.1). LLM-as-a-judge is "sensitive to the evaluator model and rubric" (§7.2.1).
- **[A] Benchmark realism limits (stated in §9.6).** User benchmarks "implicitly assume stationary user intent and unambiguous ground truth, overlooking critical failure modes such as stale preference reuse, incorrect overwriting of long-term user state, or unsafe retention of sensitive information." Agent-centric interactive benchmarks "remain bounded by curated environments, reset-centric task design, and short evaluation horizons."
- **[A] The field's understanding gap (relevant to their own agenda).** "Existing systems largely rely on heuristic memory controllers, leaving the internal coupling between memory evolution and reasoning behavior poorly understood" (§9.1).
- **[I] What is *not* acknowledged as a limitation:** the survey does not disclose its own coding reliability, exclusion criteria, or possible selection bias from venue/keyword filtering; it also does not state that many of its category assignments (substrate, mechanism, subject) are subjective. Its de facto scope limitation is only implicit in "we curated the most relevant contributions through iterative screening, ultimately selecting 218 key articles" (§3).

### (3) OPENED QUESTIONS

#### 3.1 Future work the authors explicitly call for

**Six named directions (§9):**

1. **Memory for continual learning and self-evolving agents (§9.1).** Reframe continual learning "around a richer, agent-centric notion of memory" that integrates semantic, episodic, and procedural components; study intra-task vs. cross-task memory dynamics; move "beyond inference-time heuristics toward principled post-training paradigms"; design consolidation mechanisms that "selectively distill long-term knowledge, align evolving memory with model parameters, and mitigate forgetting without sacrificing plasticity"; build benchmarks for "sustained adaptation, relevance-aware memory management, and behavioral stability under non-stationary objectives."
2. **Multi-human-agent memory organization (§9.2).** Persistent, adaptive collaboration via "collaborative (social) memory" (communication preferences, expertise, feedback patterns), role-specific procedural memory for recurring workflows, and governance questions: "ownership, access, responsibility, and how divergent perspectives or human corrections should be handled."
3. **Memory infrastructure and efficiency (§9.3).** Progression toward "organized text-based memory," "compressed latent memory," and "internalized or parametric memory" for "constant-sized memory"; joint optimization of memory and policy via "end-to-end reinforcement learning or meta-learning objectives"; build environment infrastructure such as NeMo Gym.
4. **Life-long personalization and trustworthy memory (§9.4).** Scalable dynamic user models that "bridge fine-grained episodic traces with higher-level abstractions such as preferences, habits, or long-term intents"; hierarchical buffers vs. distilled semantic profiles; learned memory controllers; continual-representation learning; and "trustworthy memory infrastructures" defending against "privacy leakage, memory poisoning, and adversarial manipulation," with "user-controllable mechanisms that support inspection, editing, and revocation."
5. **Memory for multimodal, embodied, and world-model agents (§9.5).** Treat memory "not as a passive log, but as a controllable internal state evolving over time" — i.e., memory as a predictive world model, with memory operations as "internal actions optimized jointly with external decision-making"; co-train memory and world models "in a mutually reinforcing loop."
6. **Real-world benchmarking and evaluations (§9.6).** Closed-loop, longitudinal, execution-grounded evaluation with recurring interactions and preference drift; partially open, continuously evolving environments (e.g., "financial trading sandboxes, long-running web services, or competitive control tasks with delayed payoffs"); memory-sensitive invariants (versioning, audit, rollback of persistent state); standardized tool-mediation layers (MCP-style) for reproducible logging and permission enforcement; and explicit "resource–utility trade-off" metrics.

**Smaller open problems stated in taxonomy/survey sections:**

- **[A] Episodic memory:** "Open problems include how agents should define episode boundaries, regulate the influence of episodic recall during reasoning, and manage long-term retention as episodic memory scales" (§3.2.3).
- **[A] Abstention evaluation:** Only "a few benchmarks explicitly reward abstention under missing evidence, leaving a gap for evaluating safe memory behavior that prevents confident hallucinations" (§7.2.1).
- **[A] Domain-specific calls in §8:** education — "interoperable memory protocols" so a learner profile persists across platforms; dialogue — "self-optimizing memory… agents will likely learn personalized memory policies"; robotics — "multimodal memory integration" and simulating "physical affordances based on past successes and failures"; healthcare — "privacy-preserving memory by design"; workflow — agents that "rewrite their own instructions based on long-term execution logs"; software engineering — "shared, anonymized knowledge repositories"; streaming/recommendation — "forgetting-aware recommendation memories" distinguishing "fleeting interests" from "long-term preferences"; finance — "forgetting-aware financial agents that can prune outdated economic assumptions while retaining core risk management principles"; legal — "verifiable memory architectures that link every retrieved insight back to a cryptographically signed source document."

#### 3.2 Questions their verified claims leave unanswered, which they do **not** raise

- **[I] Effect size.** How large is the memory benefit relative to other system components? The survey never quantifies (or reports others' quantifications of) the performance delta of memory-augmented vs. memory-free agents under matched compute, token, and latency budgets.
- **[I] The crossover point.** Given the acknowledged trade-offs (retrieval latency/noise vs. context growth; §3.1.1, §6.2.1), at what storage size, interaction horizon, or retrieval-noise level does explicit memory stop paying off? No threshold or cost curve is given, so a practitioner cannot use the survey to decide when to add memory vs. lengthen the context window.
- **[I] Circularity of the evaluation claim.** The authors criticize benchmarks for reporting accuracy on synthetic recall instead of "real user satisfaction" (§9.6), yet their own Table 3 mostly tabulates accuracy-based metrics. They do not address the question of how *their recommended* satisfaction-aligned memory behaviors (compression, selective forgetting, safe overwriting) would be scored without reintroducing judge bias.
- **[I] Metric–mechanism correlation.** Do LLM-judge scores and retrieval metrics (Recall@K/NDCG@K) actually agree when memory mechanisms are ablated? The survey doesn't report or cite tests of convergent validity among its three metric families.
- **[I] Atomicity of their taxonomy.** The "minimal and architecturally complete" five-mechanism decomposition (§3.2) is asserted, not tested. If prospective or autobiographical memory turns out to be functionally irreducible for agents (e.g., delayed intentions), the completeness claim is unsupported.
- **[I] Taxonomy orthogonality vs. correlation.** Figure 5/§3.3 show that cognitive mechanisms and subjects are correlated, which puts pressure on the "orthogonal" framing (§3); the survey does not investigate whether classifications are stable under re-coding.
- **[I] Under-specified training conditions.** For the RL-trained memory systems they highlight (MEM1, Mem-α, MemAgent, Memory-R1), the survey does not report compute budgets, base-model sizes, reward-shaping details, or the distribution of training tasks — leaving open whether results transfer to other agents/environments.
- **[I] Safety–utility interaction.** The survey calls for lifelong user memory AND privacy/security (§9.4) but does not discuss the empirical trade-off: richer, more useful user models may be inherently more extractable (they cite that memory modules "are vulnerable to targeted extraction attacks," §3.3.1), and no evaluated system resolves this tension.
- **[I] Whether memory is the binding constraint.** Their own §2.1 lists long-horizon reliability, evaluation, and alignment as separate "foundational challenges." The survey does not consider the possibility that failures in long-horizon settings are dominated by reward misalignment or compounding planning error rather than by missing memory.

### (4) LOAD-BEARING ASSUMPTIONS

Assumptions the survey's framing, method, or conclusions depend on, which newer evidence could invalidate.

1. **[A→I] The "utility gap" is primarily a memory problem.** The whole paper rests on: "Memory thus emerges as the critical and natural solution to bridge the gap between idealized benchmark performance and real-world implementation" (§1). Evidence that long-horizon failures are dominated by planning instability, tool-call errors, or safety misalignment (even with perfect memory) would undercut the centrality claim. The authors themselves list non-memory challenges in §2.1 (long-horizon reliability, evaluation, alignment), which creates an internal tension.
2. **[A→I] Explicit bounded context is the lasting constraint.** Memory is motivated by "context windows are limited and environments evolve over time" (§2.1), and the survey explicitly argues "rather than treating longer context windows as the sole solution" (§3.2.2). If inference costs for effectively unbounded context (or near-lossless compression/KV-cache elimination) collapse, the premium on external agent memory declines. The survey acknowledges but does not model this possibility.
3. **[A] Human cognitive categories are the correct engineering decomposition.** "These five systems constitute a minimal and architecturally complete decomposition of cognitive memory" (§3.2). Newer evidence from monolithic memory architectures (e.g., end-to-end RL systems with a single compact latent state such as MEM1, cited in §3.3.2; or "Titans"-style test-time memorization, cited in §3.2.4) could show that engineered agents do not need episodic/semantic/procedural separation to achieve long-horizon competence, making the taxonomy descriptive-analogical rather than prescriptive-mechanistic.
4. **[A→I] Taxonomy dimensions are "orthogonal" (§3).** The survey's three axes are presented as orthogonal, yet §3.3 and Figure 5 state that sensory/working/procedural memory are "predominantly agent-centric," i.e., the dimensions are empirically correlated. If subject and mechanism are confounded, the paper's two-dimensional and three-dimensional maps could overstate the structure of the design space; re-coding papers with a different convention could shift the Figure 3/5 distributions substantially.
5. **[I] Corpus representativeness.** All trend claims assume that keyword search (e.g., "agent memory," "long-term memory," "context management," "personalization memory") plus top-tier NLP/ML/IR/AI venues captures the field (§3). Research published under alternative vocabularies (e.g., "state-space models," "context distillation," "test-time training," "latent reasoning") without "memory" in the title is systematically excluded, potentially biasing the conclusion that memory-specific work is surging and that non-explicit memory mechanisms are peripheral.
